Hands-On Science

Simple Science
Experiments

Science becomes truly exciting when we can observe it in action. These three beginner-friendly experiments demonstrate real scientific principles using everyday materials.

⚠ Safety First — Important Notice

Please read these safety rules before attempting any experiment:

  • Always perform experiments under the supervision of a responsible adult or teacher.
  • Experiments involving heat, fire, chemicals, electricity, or glassware must only be conducted by qualified supervisors.
  • Never taste, smell, or touch unknown substances.
  • Keep your workspace clean and clear of trip hazards.
  • Wash hands thoroughly after completing any experiment.
01

Experiment One

Walking Water

Watch water travel through a paper towel from one glass to another — a beautiful demonstration of capillary action.

Difficulty: Easy Time: 30–60 min Concept: Capillary Action

You Will Need

  • Two glasses filled with coloured water
  • One empty glass
  • Water
  • Food colouring (two colours)
  • Paper towel

Procedure

  1. Fill two glasses with water. Add a different food colouring to each glass.
  2. Place the empty glass between the two coloured water glasses.
  3. Fold a paper towel lengthwise into a narrow strip to create a wick.
  4. Place one end of the paper towel into one coloured glass and the other end into the empty glass. Repeat with the second coloured glass.
  5. Wait patiently and observe what happens over 30 to 60 minutes.

What Happens?

The coloured water slowly "walks" up through the paper towel and drips into the empty glass. Over time, the empty glass fills up and the colours may even mix.

The Science Behind It

This happens because of capillary action — the ability of water to flow upwards through narrow spaces against gravity. Water molecules are attracted to the fibres in the paper towel, pulling the water along. This is the same force that pulls water from the roots of plants up to their leaves.

Experiment Two

Balloon and Static Electricity

Discover how rubbing a balloon can give it an electrical charge powerful enough to attract small objects.

Difficulty: Easy Time: 5 min Concept: Static Charge

You Will Need

  • A balloon (inflated)
  • Small pieces of torn paper
  • A piece of wool or a dry cloth

Procedure

  1. Inflate the balloon and tie the end securely.
  2. Tear small pieces of paper and place them flat on a table.
  3. Rub the balloon vigorously against the wool or dry cloth for about 30 seconds.
  4. Slowly bring the rubbed side of the balloon close to the paper pieces without touching them.
  5. Observe what happens to the paper.

What Happens?

The small paper pieces jump up towards the balloon and appear to stick to it, even though nothing is touching them!

The Science Behind It

When you rub the balloon against wool, electrons (negatively charged particles) transfer from the cloth to the balloon. This gives the balloon a negative static charge. The charged balloon then attracts the neutral paper pieces, because opposite charges attract each other — this is a fundamental law of electricity.

Experiment Three

The Floating Egg

Make a sinking egg float by dissolving salt in water — a demonstration of density and buoyancy.

Difficulty: Easy Time: 10 min Concept: Density

You Will Need

  • A tall glass of water
  • A fresh egg (uncooked)
  • Salt (several tablespoons)
  • A spoon for stirring

Procedure

  1. Fill a tall glass with clean, plain water.
  2. Gently lower the raw egg into the glass. Observe — it sinks.
  3. Remove the egg and add several tablespoons of salt to the water.
  4. Stir well until the salt is completely dissolved.
  5. Gently lower the egg back into the salty water and observe its position.

What Happens?

In plain water, the egg sinks to the bottom. After adding enough salt and stirring, the egg rises and floats in the middle or near the surface of the water.

The Science Behind It

This works because of density. An object floats when it is less dense than the liquid it is in. A fresh egg is denser than plain water, so it sinks. When you dissolve salt in the water, you increase the water's density. Once the salt water becomes denser than the egg, the egg floats — the same principle that makes it easier to float in a salty sea than in a freshwater pool.
